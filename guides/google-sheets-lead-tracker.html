Build a Simple Lead Tracker with Google Sheets and Make

A practical guide to collecting, reviewing and routing leads without losing track of follow-ups.

Start with a clear workflow

For a small service business, a spreadsheet can be enough to organize new enquiries. The goal is not to automate sales conversations: it is to make sure a responsible person sees each enquiry and can follow up.

Prepare your spreadsheet

Create a sheet with these headers: Created at, Lead ID, Name, Contact, Request, Owner, Status, Last reviewed. Use fictional data for testing. Assign a unique Lead ID to help prevent duplicates. Avoid storing sensitive information you do not need.

Connect Make

In Make, create a scenario with a Google Sheets module that watches for new rows. Select the appropriate spreadsheet and worksheet, and choose a starting point that avoids accidentally processing older rows. Add a filter to continue only when required fields are present.

Notify the right person

Add a supported email or team-messaging module. Map the Lead ID and Request into an internal notification. Initially, send notifications only to your own test inbox. Do not automatically contact the lead until you have confirmed consent, appropriate wording and relevant rules.

Test failure cases

Add a test row, check the scenario run history and verify that exactly one notification arrives. Then try an incomplete row and a duplicate Lead ID. Make sure these do not trigger an inappropriate follow-up. Review the impact of retries and connection failures.

Measure usefulness

Track how long it takes someone to review a lead, the number of missed enquiries and how often a notification fails. If the process is reliable, document the owner, troubleshooting steps and expected monthly usage.

Before you scale

Review access permissions, privacy requirements, and the current limits of your Make plan. A spreadsheet is not a replacement for a CRM when your team needs advanced permissions, audit history or complex sales reporting.

Build a lead-review system that your team can maintain

Use this fictional example: a two-person home-services company needs a shared list of enquiries. The owner wants to know when a new request arrives, while a staff member remains responsible for contacting the customer.

Suggested statuses: New, Reviewing, Contacted, Closed. Choose a unique ID for each enquiry and avoid storing sensitive details in the notification itself.

Configuration checklist

  1. In Google Sheets, create one header row and keep the column names stable.
  2. In Make, create a scenario with a Google Sheets watch trigger. Confirm the sheet, the starting row and how often it runs.
  3. Add a filter that requires a Lead ID, Request and Owner. If Owner is blank, route the item for manual assignment rather than guessing.
  4. Send an internal email to the responsible team inbox with the Lead ID and request summary.
  5. Keep status changes manual at first. If you later update the sheet automatically, guard against workflows that trigger themselves repeatedly.

Duplicate prevention is a design requirement

A unique Lead ID makes duplicates easier to detect, but it does not automatically prevent them. Before enabling automated customer-facing actions, implement a reliable lookup or storage mechanism and decide what should happen when the same ID appears twice. Spreadsheet formulas alone may not protect against concurrent executions.

Acceptance tests

  • Add one complete fictional lead and verify the correct internal recipient receives it.
  • Add an incomplete row and confirm it is held for review.
  • Repeat a Lead ID and confirm your chosen duplicate-handling mechanism behaves as designed.
  • Disconnect a test account and verify failures are visible in the scenario history.
  • Change a column heading in a copy of the sheet to understand how mapping failures appear.

Do not interpret these tests as completed until you have run them in your own account.

Privacy, ownership and maintenance

Limit access to the spreadsheet, use authorized Google connections, set a retention policy, and keep an owner responsible for reviewing failed runs. If your team needs advanced access controls or audit history, evaluate a CRM rather than extending the spreadsheet indefinitely.
